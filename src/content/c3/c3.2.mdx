## TL;DR

Modern language models trade off **what state they retain, which computation they activate, and how that computation reaches hardware**. Many dense decoders retain the original attention/FFN stack, with changes to normalization, positions and kernels. Others compress keys and values into latents, select sparse attention blocks, use recurrent state, or mix these mechanisms. Treat the dense decoder as a baseline to derive from, not a universal description of 2026 models.

Attach a number and a boundary to every change. In the worked 80-layer example, GQA-8 reduces a 100K-token cache from 262 GB to 33 GB. MLA stores a different object and needs a different formula. MoE lowers active computation without proportionally lowering total weight storage. FlashAttention evaluates dense attention without materializing its quadratic score matrix; algebraic equivalence does not mean bitwise equality, and low-precision paths introduce additional error.

## Part 1 — Normalization: where it goes, and what it computes

Two independent changes happened here, and they get merged into one constantly: where normalization sits relative to the residual add, and what the normalization itself computes.

### Pre-norm vs post-norm

The 2017 paper puts normalization after the sublayer and after the residual add: $x \leftarrow \text{LN}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x))$. That is post-norm. The residual stream is normalized on every hop, so the identity path is not actually an identity. Gradients flowing backward through $L$ layers get multiplied by the Jacobian of a LayerNorm at every step, and at depth those factors compound. This can make deep post-norm training sensitive to initialization and warmup; 24 layers is not a universal failure threshold.

Pre-norm moves the normalization inside the branch: $x \leftarrow x + \text{Sublayer}(\text{LN}(x))$. Now the residual path from input to output is a clean sum of branch outputs with nothing applied to it. The gradient reaches layer 1 through an unobstructed additive path, which is why pre-norm trains at depth with no warmup or a very short one. Pre-norm is common in decoder families; other normalization and residual layouts exist, and warmup requirements still depend on the training recipe.

The cost is small: pre-norm models are slightly worse at matched depth, because the residual stream grows in magnitude with depth and later layers contribute proportionally less. A final norm before unembedding controls the output scale in common pre-norm designs.

### RMSNorm

LayerNorm computes, over the $d$ features of one token,

$$
\text{LN}(x) = \gamma \odot \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} + \beta, \qquad \mu = \frac1d \sum_i x_i
$$

The index $i$ runs over those $d$ features, $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are their mean and variance, $\epsilon$ is a small constant keeping the denominator away from zero, $\odot$ is elementwise multiplication, and $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are learned per-feature gain and bias vectors. RMSNorm drops the mean subtraction and the bias:

$$
\text{RMSNorm}(x) = \gamma \odot \frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac1d \sum_i x_i^2 + \epsilon}}
$$

It keeps only the rescaling. The empirical finding is that re-centering contributes almost nothing; the variance normalization is doing the work. Dropping it removes one reduction pass over the feature vector and one parameter tensor per norm.

Why does that matter when normalization is well under 1% of a model's FLOPs? Because it is not FLOPs that cost you. A norm is an elementwise operation with very low **arithmetic intensity**, meaning FLOPs performed per byte moved from memory. Low-intensity ops are limited by memory bandwidth rather than by the arithmetic units, so their cost is measured in passes over the tensor.

A third question gets tangled with these two and belongs apart from them. The reason transformers use LayerNorm rather than BatchNorm has nothing to do with placement or with bandwidth. BatchNorm normalizes across the batch dimension, which requires batch statistics that are unstable with variable sequence lengths and poorly matched to variable-length autoregressive training. BatchNorm can use stored running statistics at inference; batch size one does not make inference mathematically impossible. LayerNorm normalizes within a token, so it behaves identically at batch 1 and batch 256.

## Part 2 — Position: from sinusoids to RoPE

Attention with no position information is **permutation-equivariant**: permute the input tokens and the output tokens come back permuted the same way, with identical values. The attention weight between positions $i$ and $j$ depends only on the content of those two tokens. So "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce the same set of representations. Position has to be injected explicitly.

The 2017 answer was fixed sinusoids added to the input embedding; GPT-2 used a learned lookup table instead. Both are **absolute**: they tag each slot with an identity, and both fail the same way. A learned table has no entry for position 5000 if you trained to 2048. Sinusoids technically define a value everywhere, but the model has never seen those phase combinations and attention degrades immediately past the training length. The sinusoid formula itself carries no weight; what matters is why position has to be injected at all and why RoPE replaced the absolute schemes.

ALiBi took a different route, adding a linear penalty $-m \cdot |i-j|$ to the attention logit between positions $i$ and $j$, where $m$ is a fixed per-head slope, so distant tokens are down-weighted. It extrapolates gracefully, but the bias is monotonic in distance and it lost to RoPE on long-range quality.

### How RoPE actually works

Rotary position embedding does not add anything to the embedding. It rotates the query and key vectors, and only those, right before the dot product. Write $d$ for the head dimension $d_{head}$, and split those $d$ coordinates into $d/2$ consecutive pairs indexed by $i = 0, 1, \dots, d/2 - 1$. Read each pair as a point in its own plane, so that "apply position" can mean "spin that point". Pair $i$ spins at its own angular frequency $\theta_i = b^{-2i/d}$, where the base $b = 10000$ sets how fast the frequencies decay; pair 0 spins fastest, pair $d/2 - 1$ slowest. A vector at position $m$ has pair $i$ turned through the angle $m\theta_i$, which is the 2x2 rotation

$$
R_m^{(i)} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos m\theta_i & -\sin m\theta_i \\ \sin m\theta_i & \cos m\theta_i \end{pmatrix}
$$

Stacking those $d/2$ blocks down a diagonal gives the single matrix $R_m$ that rotates a whole head vector at position $m$. One property then does all the work. Transposing a rotation runs it backwards, $R_m^{\top} = R_{-m}$, and rotating by $-m$ and then by $n$ is the same as rotating once by $n - m$. So for a query at position $m$ and a key at position $n$,

$$
(R_m q)^{\top} (R_n k) = q^{\top} R_m^{\top} R_n k = q^{\top} R_{n-m} k
$$

The attention logit depends on the positions only through their difference. Absolute rotations, relative scores, no extra parameters, no extra memory, and it composes with the KV cache because you apply the rotation to a key once and store the rotated key.

```python
def rope(x, pos, base=10000.0):
    # x: (batch, heads, seq, d_head), pos: (seq,)
    d = x.shape[-1]
    inv_freq = base ** (-torch.arange(0, d, 2).float() / d)   # (d/2,)
    ang = pos[:, None].float() * inv_freq[None, :]            # (seq, d/2)
    cos, sin = ang.cos(), ang.sin()
    x1, x2 = x[..., 0::2], x[..., 1::2]                       # pair the dims
    out = torch.stack([x1 * cos - x2 * sin,
                       x1 * sin + x2 * cos], dim=-1)
    return out.flatten(-2)
```

### Stretching the context after training

High-frequency pairs (small $i$, fast rotation) resolve local order; low-frequency pairs rotate slowly and encode long-range position. A model trained to 4K has never seen the low-frequency pairs complete much of a cycle, so at 32K those angles are out of distribution and attention falls apart.

Three fixes, in the order they were invented. **Position interpolation** divides every position index by the scale factor $s$, the ratio of new context length to trained length: going from 4K to 32K makes $s = 8$, so feed $m/8$ instead of $m$. Nothing is out of distribution any more, but every angle is compressed, so local resolution degrades and you need a short fine-tune. The second fix starts from the observation that only the slow dimensions were ever out of range, and so raises the base $b$ instead, which stretches the low frequencies a lot and the high frequencies barely at all. That one is called **NTK-aware scaling**, after the neural tangent kernel argument its authors used to derive it. Llama 3 shipped with $b = 500000$ for exactly this reason. **YaRN** makes that explicit: interpolate the low-frequency dimensions fully, leave the high-frequency ones alone, ramp between them, and scale attention temperature to compensate for the entropy change. All three are cheap post-hoc edits, which is why context windows grew by 30x in two years without anyone retraining from scratch.

## Part 3 — SwiGLU, and the 2/3 rule

The original feed-forward network is $W_2\,\text{ReLU}(W_1 x)$, where $d$ is the model width, $d_{ff}$ is the hidden width, $W_1$ projects a token from $d$ up to $d_{ff}$ and $W_2$ brings it back down, with $d_{ff} = 4d$. SwiGLU replaces it with a gated form using three matrices:

$$
\text{SwiGLU}(x) = W_2 \big( \text{SiLU}(W_g x) \odot (W_1 x) \big), \qquad \text{SiLU}(z) = z\,\sigma(z)
$$

Here $W_g$ is a third up-projection of the same shape as $W_1$, $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid, and $\odot$ is again elementwise multiplication. One branch produces values, the other a multiplicative gate, and the elementwise product gives the layer a cheap second-order interaction that a single matmul plus pointwise nonlinearity cannot express. It is worth roughly a percent of loss at matched parameters, which is a lot for a one-line change.

Three matrices instead of two would be a 50% parameter increase at the same $d_{ff}$, so implementations shrink $d_{ff}$ to compensate. Match the parameter count: $3 d\, d_{ff} = 8d^2$ gives $d_{ff} = \frac{8}{3}d$. For $d = 4096$ that is 10923, and Llama ships 11008 (the nearest multiple of 256 for kernel alignment). The name comes from the ratio to the old width: $\frac83 d$ is $\frac23$ of $4d$. If a model card shows an FFN width that is an odd non-power-of-two like 11008 or 14336, that is the 2/3 rule and a SwiGLU.

## Part 4 — GQA and MQA: compressing the KV cache

The single most common point of confusion is which heads get shared, so state it precisely. `n_heads` is the number of **query** heads. `n_kv_heads` is the number of distinct key/value head pairs. Multi-head attention sets them equal. **Multi-query attention** sets `n_kv_heads = 1`: all query heads attend against one shared key and one shared value. **Grouped-query attention** sets it to something in between, typically 8, and each group of query heads shares a KV head. Only the KV heads are cached, so the cache shrinks by exactly `n_heads / n_kv_heads`.

The cache size formula, which you should be able to write without thinking:

$$
\text{bytes} = 2 \times n_{layers} \times n_{kv} \times d_{head} \times \text{seq} \times \text{batch} \times \text{dtype bytes}
$$

The leading 2 is keys and values. $n_{layers}$ is the number of layers, $n_{kv}$ is `n_kv_heads`, $d_{head}$ is the dimension of one head, seq is the sequence length in tokens and batch is the number of sequences held at once. Dtype bytes: 4 for fp32, 2 for fp16 or bf16, 1 for fp8 or int8.

Worked example, a 70B-class model: 80 layers, 64 query heads, $d_{head} = 128$, so $d_{model} = 8192$. Weights in fp16 are about 140 GB. One sequence at 100K context:

- **MHA** ($n_{kv} = 64$): $2 \times 80 \times 64 \times 128 \times 10^5 \times 2 = 2.62 \times 10^{11}$ bytes, about **262 GB**. The cache for one user is larger than the entire model.
- **GQA-8**: divide by 8, about **33 GB**.
- **MQA**: divide by 64, about **4.1 GB**.

As an optimistic capacity bound, an 8×80 GB node minus 140 GB of weights leaves 500 GB. That fits one MHA request or about fifteen GQA-8 requests at 100K tokens, before activations, workspace, fragmentation and sharding constraints. This explains the incentive to reduce cache size; it does not establish that every large model uses GQA.

Sharing KV heads reduces representational freedom. The quality tradeoff depends on architecture, training and task; eight KV heads is an example, not a universal optimum. [Ainslie et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13245) study GQA as a compromise between MHA and MQA. MLA pursues cache compression with a different representation.

### MLA: cache a latent, not fewer heads

**Goal — derive memory from the tensor actually retained.** In [DeepSeek-V2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434), a token's hidden state is projected into a compressed KV latent $c_t^{KV}$ of width $d_c$. Content keys and values are reconstructed from that latent. In an optimized decode path, the key projection can be absorbed into the query projection and the value projection applied after accumulation, avoiding a permanently expanded cache. A separate positional key of width $d_R$ handles decoupled RoPE.

For $L$ layers, sequence length $s$, batch $B$ and $b$ bytes per element, that compressed cache costs

$$\text{bytes}_{MLA}=L s B(d_c+d_R)b.$$

There is no leading factor of two for separate full K and V tensors. Hold the earlier example's $L=80$, $s=100{,}000$, $B=1$, $b=2$ fixed and choose illustrative $d_c=512$, $d_R=64$: $80\times100{,}000\times576\times2=9.216$ GB, versus 32.768 GB for GQA-8. That is a 3.56× cache reduction for these dimensions, not a quality-matched comparison of actual models. Materializing expanded K/V, padding or replication changes the allocation.

## Part 5 — Mixture of experts

Replace the single FFN in a block with $E$ parallel FFNs and a small router, a linear layer producing $E$ logits per token. Take the top $k$ (usually 2), softmax over those $k$ scores, and sum the chosen experts' outputs weighted by them. Every token takes a different pair of experts, and routing is per token per layer, not per sequence.

Total parameters and per-token FLOPs decouple. Mixtral 8x7B has 46.7B parameters and activates about 12.9B per token. A forward pass costs roughly $2N$ FLOPs per token when $N$ parameters are active, so Mixtral costs like a 13B model and stores like a 47B one. In fp16 that is 94 GB of weights you must hold resident versus 26 GB for the dense 13B whose active arithmetic is comparable; realized speed also depends on routing and hardware.

Sparse routing preserves its per-token FLOP saving even if a large batch collectively visits every expert: each token still activates only its selected experts. Total resident weights, expert load imbalance, all-to-all communication and small expert matrix multiplies determine achieved latency and throughput. Larger batches can improve expert utilization while also touching more weight bytes. Offloading experts trades GPU memory for transfer delay; full residency is a common performance choice, not a mathematical requirement. MoE changes compute, storage and communication differently.

Routing has a stability problem. The router is trained jointly, so an expert that gets picked slightly more early on gets more gradient, gets better, and gets picked more. Left alone this collapses to a few overloaded experts while the rest are dead weight. The standard fix is an auxiliary load-balancing loss, added to the training objective:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{aux} = \alpha E \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i \, P_i
$$

where $\alpha$ is a small coefficient setting how hard the term pushes, $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens in the batch routed to expert $i$, and $P_i$ is the mean router probability for expert $i$ over that same batch. The product is minimized when both are uniform. $P_i$ has to appear alongside $f_i$ because $f_i$ comes out of a hard top-$k$ selection and carries no gradient, while $P_i$ does. Implementations also set a **capacity factor**, a hard cap on tokens per expert per batch, and tokens over the cap are dropped (their residual passes through unchanged). Newer models bias the router logits directly based on recent load instead of adding a loss term, which avoids the auxiliary gradient perturbing quality.

### A modern MoE is a systems recipe

**Goal — separate sparse arithmetic from the other sources of efficiency.** [DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437) has 671B total parameters and activates about 37B per token. Its router-bias load balancing reduces dependence on a large auxiliary balancing loss; the report still includes a sequence-wise auxiliary term. Its **FP8 mixed-precision training** changes selected arithmetic and storage during training, unlike quantizing a finished model's weights. Its **multi-token prediction** objective trains prediction of additional future tokens; that is distinct from the accept/reject verification that makes speculative decoding distribution-preserving. Low active parameter count alone explains none of those three mechanisms.

Measure expert imbalance and inter-device traffic alongside FLOPs. Two MoE deployments with identical active parameters can differ substantially in throughput because tokens and experts are placed differently.

## Part 6 — FlashAttention is exact

Two memory tiers matter on a GPU. **HBM** is the high-bandwidth off-chip memory, tens of gigabytes at a few TB/s. **SRAM** is on-chip scratchpad, roughly 20 MB total, one to two orders of magnitude faster. Every read from HBM is expensive relative to arithmetic.

Naive attention computes the score matrix $S = QK^{\top}$ for a sequence of $n$ tokens, writes that full $n \times n$ matrix to HBM, reads it back to softmax it, writes it again, reads it again to multiply by the value matrix $V$. At $n = 100000$ in fp16 that intermediate is $10^{10} \times 2 = 20$ GB per head per sequence. Attention is $O(n^2)$ in memory, not just in time, and that is the wall people hit first.

FlashAttention tiles $Q$, $K$ and $V$ into blocks that fit in SRAM and never materializes $S$. For each block of queries it walks over the key blocks, maintaining a running maximum $m$ of the row's scores so far (a different $m$ from the position index of Part 2), a running sum $\ell$ of the exponentials that form the softmax denominator, and a running output accumulator. It rescales on each step using the online-softmax identity: when the running maximum increases from $m$ to a new value $m'$, multiply the accumulated numerator and denominator by $e^{m - m'}$ before adding the new block's contribution. Everything accumulated so far was exponentiated relative to the old maximum, and that single factor re-bases it to the new one, so the running totals stay exactly what a full-matrix softmax would have produced. The final result is algebraically identical to the standard computation. Backward recomputes the tiles from the stored $m$ and $\ell$ rather than reading a saved attention matrix.

For the dense, full-precision algorithm, “exact” means the same attention operation in real arithmetic, not bitwise-identical floating-point results. The original IO bound is $O(n^2d^2/M)$ HBM accesses under its memory-model assumptions, with head width $d$ and SRAM capacity $M$ measured in elements, versus materializing $O(n^2)$ scores. Fixed-width peak auxiliary memory becomes linear in sequence length. Arithmetic remains quadratic; backward recomputation can add FLOPs. [FlashAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135) is an IO algorithm, not a sparse-attention approximation.

### Hardware changes the bottleneck

**Goal — explain why a faster matrix unit does not automatically make the whole kernel faster.** [FlashAttention-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08608) targets Hopper with asynchronous execution and overlap between data movement, matrix multiplication and softmax. Its FP8 path must also manage quantization error; the dense-attention equivalence claim does not erase that error.

[FlashAttention-4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05451), a March 2026 preprint, targets Blackwell's uneven scaling: tensor cores improved faster than exponential units and shared-memory bandwidth. It changes pipelines, softmax work and memory traffic accordingly. A speedup belongs to a GPU, dtype, sequence shape and comparison kernel. Rebenchmark those together; an H100 calculation remains a useful worked example, not a description of every current deployment.

## Part 7 — Tokenization leaks into behavior

Byte-pair encoding starts with a base vocabulary, counts adjacent symbol pairs across the corpus, merges the most frequent pair into a new symbol, and repeats until the vocabulary reaches the target size. The merge list is the tokenizer. Encoding replays the merges in order.

The 2017 model used word-piece style vocabularies with an unknown token. GPT-2's change was **byte-level** BPE: the base alphabet is the 256 byte values, so every possible input string is representable and there is no `UNK` case, ever. Modern tokenizers use several families, including byte-level BPE and unigram models with byte fallback; inspect the specific tokenizer rather than assuming its algorithm.

Why an application engineer should care:

1. **Cost and latency scale with tokens, not characters.** The same paragraph in Hindi or Thai can cost two to five times the tokens of its English translation, because the merges were learned on an English-heavy corpus. That multiplies your API bill, multiplies time-to-first-token, and divides your effective context window, all by the same factor. It is a fairness problem and a budget problem at the same time.
2. **Arithmetic is a tokenization artifact.** If "1234" is one token and "12345" is a different single token, the model has no positional view of the digits. Digit grouping differs by model family and tokenizer version. Splitting digits exposes place-level structure but also lengthens the sequence; evaluate arithmetic and token cost together.
3. **Character-level questions are unanswerable by construction.** "How many r's in strawberry" is hard because the model sees three opaque token IDs, not letters.
4. **Domain mismatch fragments your vocabulary.** Legal and medical text, chemical names and code with long identifiers all shatter into many tokens under a general tokenizer, inflating cost and weakening the model's representation of the terms that matter most in that domain.

One more piece of arithmetic, because vocabulary size lands in two places and the second one catches people out. "A vocabulary of 100K, how would you optimize" is a question people report being asked directly. With $d = 4096$ for the model width and a 128K vocabulary, the embedding matrix is $128000 \times 4096 = 524$M parameters, about 1 GB in fp16, and the unembedding is another one unless you tie them. Worse, the logits tensor during prefill of an 8K prompt is $8192 \times 128000 \times 2$ bytes, about 2.1 GB, for one sequence. That is why production decode computes logits only at the final position, and why chunked prefill and fused cross-entropy kernels exist.

## Part 8 — Dense, sparse, recurrent, or hybrid

**Goal — distinguish changing the model's state from changing the kernel evaluating it.** [Mamba-2 / Transformers are SSMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21060) develops structured state-space duality and an efficient selective state-space layer. Recurrent decoding carries a fixed-size state per layer as sequence length grows. That trades direct access to every old token for a compressed evolving state. The equivalence concerns structured attention variants, not arbitrary softmax attention, and layer speedups do not establish universal model quality.

| Mechanism | Retained decode state as context grows | Work or information it changes | What to measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dense attention + FlashAttention | KV cache grows linearly | Kernel IO; preserves dense attention | Memory, latency, numerical error |
| MLA | Compressed latent cache grows linearly | KV representation | Actual cache layout and task quality |
| Native sparse attention | Implementation-dependent cache; sparsity alone does not guarantee bounded storage | Which positions or compressed blocks are attended to | Evidence recall and achieved kernel speed |
| Recurrent SSM | Fixed-size recurrent state for fixed architecture | How history is compressed | Long-range retrieval and state capacity |
| Hybrid SSM/attention | Recurrent state plus KV for attention layers | Layer-by-layer mix | Remaining attention-cache growth and quality |

[Native Sparse Attention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11089) combines coarse compression, fine block selection and a local sliding window, trained end to end. It changes the attention computation; FlashAttention accelerates dense attention. Fewer nominal FLOPs help only if selection and memory movement also fit the hardware. Test exact-evidence retrieval as well as throughput: a fast model that cannot recover the required span fails the workload.

### Emerging architecture notes, reviewed September 2026

**Goal — locate new mechanisms without treating preprints as settled defaults.** [Engram](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07372) (January 2026, revised July) adds conditional lookup memory for local token patterns, complementing conditional computation in its experiments. This is learned architectural memory, distinct from document RAG and an agent's editable notes. Deterministic addressing can support prefetch, but its gains depend on the tested model and resource allocation.

[mHC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24880) first appeared on **31 December 2025**, with a January 2026 revision. It constrains mixing across expanded residual streams to improve stability and scalability. It changes residual connectivity, not KV caching. These are designs to understand and evaluate, not assumptions to silently substitute into the baseline block.

## Tradeoff matrix

| Change | Replaces | Buys you | Costs you | The number |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-norm | Post-norm | trains at depth without warmup | slightly worse at matched depth | 100-plus layers stable |
| RMSNorm | LayerNorm | one fewer reduction, no bias params | none measurable | a few percent of a bandwidth-bound op |
| RoPE | Learned / sinusoidal absolute PE | relative distances, extendable post-hoc | applied every layer to Q and K | 4K to 128K with no retrain |
| SwiGLU | ReLU FFN | gating, better loss at equal params | three matmuls instead of two | $d_{ff} = \frac83 d$, e.g. 11008 |
| GQA-8 | MHA | 8x smaller KV cache | small quality loss | 262 GB to 33 GB at 100K |
| MQA | MHA | 64x smaller KV cache | real quality loss, training instability | 262 GB to 4 GB |
| MoE top-2 | Dense FFN | 3.6x fewer FLOPs per token | same VRAM, routing complexity | 47B stored, 13B active |
| FlashAttention | Naive attention kernel | $O(n)$ memory, 2-4x faster | none, it is exact | 20 GB intermediate to zero |
| Byte-level BPE | Word-level with UNK | no unknown tokens | non-English token inflation | 2-5x tokens for some languages |

## Production failure modes

1. **A long-context model that quietly degrades past its advertised window.** RoPE scaling was applied without fine-tuning, or the serving stack's scaling config does not match the checkpoint's. Retrieval accuracy collapses in the middle of the context while perplexity looks fine.
2. **KV cache OOM under concurrency, not under length.** Capacity was sized from a single-request test. Cache is linear in batch and in sequence, so 15 users at 30K tokens is the same pressure as one user at 450K, and the failure arrives as a burst of evictions or 500s.
3. **Deploying an MoE on hardware sized by active parameters alone.** Provisioning for 12.9B active parameters does not allocate space for a 46.7B-parameter checkpoint. Size total weights and KV state; if using expert offload, include its transfer latency and throughput in the capacity test.

## Interview gotchas

- **"Why RoPE over sinusoidal or learned position embeddings?"** Because the logit depends only on $n - m$: $(R_m q)^{\top}(R_n k) = q^{\top} R_{n-m} k$. Relative by construction, no parameters, and the frequency structure lets you interpolate or rebase to extend context after training. Learned embeddings have no value at all at unseen positions.
- **"Is FlashAttention an approximation?"** No. It is exact and it does not reduce FLOPs. It reduces HBM reads and writes by tiling into SRAM with an online softmax, and peak memory from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$.
- **"GQA vs MQA at 100K context, how much do you save?"** Cache scales as $n_{kv}$. For a 70B with 64 heads and 80 layers, MHA is 262 GB per sequence in fp16, GQA-8 is 33 GB, MQA is 4 GB. These are illustrative dimensions. Choose KV sharing jointly with task quality and the trained architecture; eight heads is not a universal optimum.
- **"Does MoE reduce memory?"** Sparse routing reduces active arithmetic, not total checkpoint size. Residency or offload determines GPU memory. Each token remains sparse even when a batch visits every expert; routing balance, expert utilization and communication determine achieved latency.
